# Teacher Script: A Train Crossing a Bridge

- Grade: Grade 4
- Lesson path: `lessons/火车过桥/index.html`
- Suggested duration: 12–15 minutes
- Default values: train length 800 m, bridge length 1600 m, distance travelled each second 40 m
- Teacher cues: The **Action** notes are for the teacher and are not meant to be read aloud. Ask and pause before selecting **Next**. On motion pages, the teacher controls **Play motion / Pause / Replay**. The imaginary walker is only a speed model; 40 metres per second is not a realistic running speed.

## Stop 1: Read

**Action**

Open the lesson and remain on page 1, **Read**. Point to the train length, bridge length, distance travelled each second, and the two timing moments.

**Ask**

What information do we know? When does the timing start, and when does it finish?

**Pause**

Wait for students to identify 800 m, 1600 m, and 40 m each second. Then wait for “the front enters” and “the rear completely leaves.”

**Say**

The train is 800 metres long. It travels 40 metres each second and must cross a bridge 1600 metres long. We start timing when the front enters the bridge and stop only when the rear has completely left it. For now, we are reading carefully rather than calculating.

**Screen check**

The stage shows the complete problem, train, bridge, all three values, both timing moments, and the opening question. No method or final answer is visible.

## Stop 2: At start

**Action**

Select **Next** to open page 2, **At start**. Point to both ends of the bridge, the walker at 0 m, and the speed sign.

**Ask**

If this walker starts at the entrance and moves 40 metres each second, how long will the whole bridge take?

**Pause**

Let students predict, then wait for them to say that each second adds another 40 metres.

**Say**

We will begin with an imaginary experiment. This is not a realistic running speed. Treat the walker as one position. The walker starts at 0 metres and must reach the far end at 1600 metres.

**Screen check**

The same complete bridge is visible. The walker stands at 0 m, the sign says “Every 1 second, move 40 m,” and no division is shown yet.

## Stop 3: Walker moves

**Action**

Select **Next** to open page 3, **Walker moves**. Make sure students can see the whole bridge, then select **Play motion**. Select **Pause** midway if useful.

**Ask**

How long has the walker travelled? How far has the walker gone? How are the two counters changing together?

**Pause**

Pause the motion and ask students to read both counters and check that distance equals time multiplied by 40.

**Say**

We keep the same complete bridge in view; there is no close-up. Every extra second adds 40 metres. For example, 10 seconds gives 400 metres and 20 seconds gives 800 metres. Continue until the walker reaches the far end.

**Screen check**

The walker moves from the entrance to the far end. Elapsed time grows from 0 s to 40 s and distance from 0 m to 1600 m. The control changes from **Play motion** to **Replay** at the end.

## Stop 4: Walker arrives

**Action**

Select **Next** to open page 4, **Walker arrives**. Point to the walker at the far end, the complete 1600-metre path, and the speed sign.

**Ask**

How many “40 metres travelled in one second” parts fit inside 1600 metres? What does each part represent?

**Pause**

Wait for “forty 40-metre parts, each representing 1 second, so 40 seconds altogether.”

**Say**

Only now do we write the motion as division. Dividing 1600 by 40 counts how many one-second 40-metre parts fit inside the full distance. The quotient is 40, meaning 40 one-second parts, or 40 seconds. This is why time equals distance divided by distance travelled each second.

**Screen check**

The walker is at the far end of the complete bridge. The working shows “Time = number of one-second distances,” “Time = distance ÷ distance travelled each second,” and “1600 ÷ 40 = 40 s.”

**Conditional branch: the values do not divide evenly**

Read only the matching screen branch. Count the complete one-second blocks, then identify the remaining distance that takes less than 1 second. The page displays the time to at most two decimal places.

## Stop 5: Before

**Action**

Select **Next** to open page 5, **Before**. Point to the front, rear, bridge entrance, and far end.

**Ask**

What is the same as the walker, and what is different?

**Pause**

Wait for “the same start and speed,” and “the train has a length of 800 metres.”

**Say**

Now replace the walker with the train. The front also starts at the bridge entrance and travels 40 metres each second. But our walker was one position, while the train is 800 metres long. We must watch both the front and the rear.

**Screen check**

The whole train is outside the bridge, its front is exactly at the entrance, and the rear marker is at the other end of the train. The working shows the 800-metre train length.

## Stop 6: Crossing

**Action**

Select **Next** to open page 6, **Crossing**. Select **Play motion** and watch the train move along the same complete bridge.

**Ask**

When the front reaches the far end, has the whole train crossed?

**Pause**

When the motion ends, pause and ask students to locate the front and then the rear before answering.

**Say**

Like the walker, the front travels 1600 metres from the entrance to the far end in 40 seconds. Now look at the rear: it is still on the bridge. The front reaching the far end is only an intermediate moment.

**Screen check**

A pale train remains at the front-at-entrance starting position while the solid train moves to front-at-far-end. The counters finish at 40 s and 1600 m, and the rear is still visibly on the bridge.

## Stop 7: Check rear

**Action**

Select **Next** to open page 7, **Check rear**. Point to the front at the far end, then follow the gold line back to the rear.

**Ask**

How much farther must the rear travel before it reaches the far end?

**Pause**

Wait for students to identify one whole train length.

**Say**

The front and rear are exactly one train length apart. Although the front has reached the far end, the rear is still on the bridge. The whole train must move another 800 metres before the rear reaches the far end.

**Screen check**

The complete solid train remains in the previous page's ending position: the front marker is at the far end, the rear marker remains on the bridge, and the gold measure reads “800 m remains.”

## Stop 8: Rear leaves

**Action**

Select **Next** to open page 8, **Rear leaves**. Point to the pale previous position, then select **Play motion** and focus on the rear of the solid train.

**Ask**

Where will the rear be after the train moves another 800 metres? How many more seconds will that take?

**Pause**

Pause midway if useful and ask students to read the extra-time and extra-distance counters. At the end, ask them to confirm the rear’s position.

**Say**

The pale train preserves the moment when the front reached the far end. The solid train moves another 800 metres, exactly one train length. Eight hundred divided by 40 is 20 seconds. At the end, the rear reaches the far end and the whole train has left the bridge.

**Screen check**

Extra time grows from 0 s to 20 s and extra distance from 0 m to 800 m. The pale starting position remains visible, and the rear finishes exactly at the far end.

**Conditional branch: the extra distance is not a whole number of seconds**

Count the complete one-second blocks and the remaining distance. Then read the exact decimal or approximate time currently shown on screen.

## Stop 9: Verify

**Action**

Select **Next** to open page 9, **Verify**. First point to the complete bridge and the train with its rear just beyond the far end. Then point to the teal bridge part, gold train part, complete distance, and answer area below.

**Ask**

Why is the complete distance the bridge length plus the train length? How can multiplication check the time?

**Pause**

Wait for “the front covers the bridge and the rear needs one more train length.” Then ask students to calculate 60 × 40.

**Say**

In the final position, the rear has just left the far end and the whole train is beyond the bridge. The front first travels the 1600-metre bridge, and the rear then needs another 800 metres. The complete distance is 1600 plus 800, or 2400 metres. There are 60 groups of 40 metres in 2400 metres, so the time is 60 seconds. Multiplying back, 60 times 40 equals 2400, exactly the bridge length plus the train length.

**Screen check**

The top of the final page retains the complete bridge and the train with its rear just leaving. “Bridge 1600 m” aligns exactly below the bridge, and “Train 800 m” aligns exactly below the train. The page also shows “1600 + 800 = 2400 m,” “(1600 + 800) ÷ 40 = 60 s,” “60 × 40 = 2400 m,” and “Answer: It takes 60 s.”

**Conditional branch**

For an exact decimal, multiply that decimal by the speed to verify the complete distance. When the page says “about,” explain that the displayed time has been rounded to at most two decimal places and read the approximate answer shown.
