# How to Rent Vehicles for the Lowest Cost — English Speaker Script

Grade level: Grade 4  
Lesson page: `lessons/租车怎样最省钱/index.html`  
Suggested length: 12–15 minutes  
Default problem: 15 teachers and 355 students; large vehicle: 40 seats and 720 per vehicle; small vehicle: 25 seats and 500 per vehicle.

> Printing note: Text under “Say” may be read aloud directly. “Action,” “Ask,” “Pause,” and “Screen check” are teacher cues and should not be read aloud.

## Before the lesson

**Action**

1. Open the lesson page.
2. Check the permanent problem strip at the top: 15 teachers, 355 students, 370 people in total; large vehicle 40 seats/720; small vehicle 25 seats/500.
3. Click the language button in the upper-right corner to switch the page to English.
4. Stay on the “Read” step. Do not open the final answer yet.

---

## Stop 1: Read the problem

**Action**

Stay on “Read.” Point to the permanent problem strip and the people and vehicle information on the page.

**Say**

Our school is planning a trip. Fifteen teachers and three hundred fifty-five students need vehicles.

A large vehicle holds forty people and costs seven hundred twenty. A small vehicle holds twenty-five people and costs five hundred.

Our question is: how can everyone get a seat while the school pays the lowest possible price?

**Ask**

What information does the problem give us? What do we need to find?

**Pause**

Wait for students to identify the number of people, the seat capacities, the prices, and the goal of finding the lowest cost.

**Action**

Click “Count everyone.”

---

## Stop 2: Count everyone

**Ask**

Teachers and students all need seats. How can we find the total number of people?

**Pause**

Wait for: add fifteen and three hundred fifty-five.

**Action**

Click “Reveal.”

**Say**

Teachers and students all travel together, so we add the two groups.

Fifteen plus three hundred fifty-five equals three hundred seventy. Three hundred seventy people need seats.

Every plan we test must have at least three hundred seventy seats. Even one missing seat means the plan does not work.

**Screen check**

The page should show: `15 + 355 = 370`.

**Action**

Click “Next.”

---

## Stop 3: Try the two single-vehicle plans

**Say**

We should not assume that large vehicles are always cheaper. We should not assume that small vehicles are always cheaper either.

First, let us calculate two simple plans: use only large vehicles, and use only small vehicles. Then we can compare them.

**Ask**

How many large vehicles would we need? How many small vehicles would we need?

**Action**

Click “Reveal.”

**Say — use this for the default small-vehicle price of 500**

First, look at the large vehicles. Three hundred seventy divided by forty is nine with ten people left over. Nine large vehicles only hold three hundred sixty people, so we need one more. We need ten large vehicles.

Ten times seven hundred twenty is seven thousand two hundred.

Now look at the small vehicles. Three hundred seventy divided by twenty-five is fourteen with twenty people left over. Fourteen small vehicles are not enough, so we need fifteen.

Fifteen times five hundred is seven thousand five hundred.

Seven thousand two hundred is less than seven thousand five hundred. Of these two plans, the all-large plan is cheaper for now. We will use it as an orderly starting point and replace one vehicle at a time.

The words “cheaper for now” are important. We have not proved that it is the final answer. A mixed plan may cost even less.

**Screen check**

The page should show 7,200 for all large vehicles, 7,500 for all small vehicles, and a message that the all-large plan is the starting point.

### If the small-vehicle price is changed to 300

Read this paragraph instead:

The all-large plan still needs ten vehicles and costs seven thousand two hundred. The all-small plan needs fifteen vehicles. Fifteen times three hundred is four thousand five hundred.

Four thousand five hundred is less than seven thousand two hundred, so the all-small plan is cheaper for now. We should start with fifteen small vehicles, remove one small vehicle at a time, and add only enough large vehicles to replace the missing seats.

This only chooses an orderly starting point. It does not assume the final answer.

**Action**

Click “Next.”

## Stop 4: Use one fewer starting vehicle

### Default price 500: start with large vehicles

**Say**

Change ten large vehicles to nine large vehicles.

Nine times forty is three hundred sixty seats. We need three hundred seventy seats, so ten seats are missing.

One small vehicle has twenty-five seats, so one small vehicle is enough to fill the gap.

**Ask**

How many seats do nine large vehicles and one small vehicle provide? How many seats will be empty?

**Action**

Click “Reveal.”

**Say**

Three hundred sixty plus twenty-five is three hundred eighty-five seats. Everyone can sit, and fifteen seats are empty.

The cost is nine times seven hundred twenty, plus one times five hundred. That equals six thousand nine hundred eighty.

This is two hundred twenty less than the all-large plan. A mixed plan can be cheaper, but this is only the lowest price we have seen so far.

### Small-vehicle price 300: start with small vehicles

Read this paragraph instead:

Change fifteen small vehicles to fourteen small vehicles. Fourteen times twenty-five is three hundred fifty seats, so twenty seats are missing.

One large vehicle has forty seats, so one large vehicle is enough. The cost is one times seven hundred twenty, plus fourteen times three hundred. That equals four thousand nine hundred twenty.

Four thousand nine hundred twenty is more than the all-small cost of four thousand five hundred. The all-small plan is still the cheapest so far, but we must continue checking every case.

**Action**

Click “Next.”

## Stop 5: Use one fewer again

### Default price 500

**Say**

Now try eight large vehicles.

Eight times forty is three hundred twenty seats. Three hundred seventy minus three hundred twenty is fifty, so fifty seats are missing.

Each small vehicle has twenty-five seats. Fifty divided by twenty-five is exactly two, so we need two small vehicles.

**Action**

Click “Reveal.”

**Say**

Eight large vehicles and two small vehicles provide exactly three hundred seventy seats. There are no empty seats.

The cost is eight times seven hundred twenty, plus two times five hundred. That equals six thousand seven hundred sixty.

This is lower than six thousand nine hundred eighty, so it is the lowest price we have seen so far. However, “lowest so far” does not yet mean “certainly the lowest.” We have more cases to check.

### Small-vehicle price 300

Read this paragraph instead:

Now try thirteen small vehicles. Thirteen times twenty-five is three hundred twenty-five seats, so forty-five seats are missing.

One large vehicle only adds forty seats, which is not enough. We need two large vehicles.

The cost is two times seven hundred twenty, plus thirteen times three hundred. That equals five thousand three hundred forty. The all-small plan at four thousand five hundred is still cheaper.

We must list every remaining small-vehicle count before we can be certain.

**Action**

Click “Next.”

## Stop 6: Compare every plan

**Say**

Testing only two or three plans cannot prove which plan is cheapest. Now we will use the same method to list every remaining case.

With the default prices, the number of large vehicles goes from ten all the way to zero. For each large-vehicle count, we use only the minimum number of small vehicles needed for enough seats.

**Ask**

Look at the price bars. Which plan has the lowest price?

**Pause**

Let students observe and answer before revealing the highlight.

**Action**

Click “Reveal.”

**Say — default price 500**

The lowest bar is eight large vehicles and two small vehicles. The cost is six thousand seven hundred sixty.

We did not choose only a few promising plans. We tested every large-vehicle count from ten to zero, so no large-vehicle count was missed.

**Say — small-vehicle price 300**

If the small vehicle costs three hundred, the lowest bar is zero large vehicles and fifteen small vehicles. The cost is four thousand five hundred.

In this version, the page lists every small-vehicle count from fifteen to zero. For each count, it adds only the minimum number of large vehicles needed.

**Optional action**

Click any price bar. Ask students to check the seats, empty seats, and cost using the calculation shown below the chart.

**Action**

Click “Next.”

## Stop 7: Explain why the price is certainly the lowest

**Ask**

How can we prove that the lowest price was not found by accident? How do we know there is no cheaper plan left out?

**Action**

Click “Reveal.”

**Say — default price 500**

First, every large-vehicle count from ten to zero was tested, so no case was missed.

Second, for each large-vehicle count, we used only enough small vehicles for everyone to sit. One extra small vehicle would add five hundred to the cost, so it could not make the plan cheaper.

Third, we compared every price, and six thousand seven hundred sixty was the lowest.

Therefore, eight large vehicles and two small vehicles are not merely cheaper than a few examples. They form the cheapest plan among all possible plans.

**Say — small-vehicle price 300**

If the small vehicle costs three hundred, every small-vehicle count from fifteen to zero was tested.

For each small-vehicle count, only enough large vehicles were added. One extra large vehicle would add seven hundred twenty to the cost.

After comparing all prices, four thousand five hundred is the lowest. Therefore, fifteen small vehicles are the cheapest plan.

**Action**

Click “Next.”

## Stop 8: State the complete answer

**Say — default price 500**

Now we can give the complete answer.

Rent eight large vehicles and two small vehicles. This is the cheapest plan, and the minimum cost is six thousand seven hundred sixty.

The calculation is eight times seven hundred twenty, plus two times five hundred, which equals six thousand seven hundred sixty.

**Say — small-vehicle price 300**

If the small vehicle costs three hundred, say:

Rent fifteen small vehicles. This is the cheapest plan, and the minimum cost is four thousand five hundred.

The calculation is fifteen times three hundred, which equals four thousand five hundred.

**Ask**

Why can we not test only one or two plans and immediately claim that one is the cheapest?

**Closing statement**

The words “lowest cost” mean that everyone must have a seat, every possible case must be considered in an orderly way, and all the valid prices must be compared.
