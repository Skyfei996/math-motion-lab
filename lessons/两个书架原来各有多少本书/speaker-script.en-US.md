# How Many Books Did Each Shelf Start With? — Teacher Script

- Grade: 4
- Lesson path: `lessons/两个书架原来各有多少本书/index.html`
- Suggested duration: 12–15 minutes
- Default values: take 200 books from the first shelf, add 40 books to the second shelf, and the second shelf then has 3 times as many
- Teacher controls: click the open stage or “Next”; Space, Arrow Right, and PageDown move forward; Arrow Left and PageUp move back
- Teaching rule: Ask and pause before reading “Say.” Bold labels are teacher cues. After editing the problem, use the numbers currently shown on screen.

## Stop 1: Read the whole problem

**Action**

Stay on the opening screen. Point to the two equal starting shelves, “Take out 200,” “Add 40,” and the 3-times relationship in the problem.

**Ask**

What facts do we know, and what must we find?

**Pause**

Wait for students to identify the equal start, both changes, the final multiple, and the unknown starting amount.

**Say**

The shelves start with the same number of books, but that number is unknown. The first shelf loses 200 books, and the second gains 40. After both changes, the second shelf has three times as many as the first. We will find the changed amounts first, then work backward.

**Screen check**

Two bookcases sit side by side with shells about 1.7 times as wide as they are high. Their starting counts are unknown. The numbers 240, 120, and 320 are not visible.

## Stop 2: Take 200 from the first shelf

**Action**

Click Next. Point to the hatched empty space that opens above the remaining books on the first shelf. Emphasize that the bookcase stays still while only the books change.

**Ask**

What changes when 200 books leave the first shelf?

**Pause**

Wait for students to describe a decrease and the empty space left behind.

**Say**

The shelves began equal. Removing 200 books makes the first shelf smaller by 200. The hatching marks the space left by the books that were removed.

**Screen check**

The first bookcase shows hatched empty space, while the second keeps its starting amount. No answer is visible.

## Stop 3: Add 40 to the second shelf

**Action**

Click Next. Point to the new gold books at the top of the second shelf, then to the 1-to-3 relationship in the middle.

**Ask**

How does adding 40 books to the second shelf change the gap?

**Pause**

Wait for students to explain that the gap grows again.

**Say**

The first shelf has already lost 200 books. The second now gains 40 more. One side decreases while the other increases, so the distance between them grows from both directions.

**Screen check**

The first shelf keeps its hatched space, and a gold section appears at the top of the second shelf. Both bookcase shells remain fixed.

## Stop 4: Combine the changes into a 240-book gap

**Action**

Click Next. Point first to the removed section, then to the added section, and finally trace the middle brace.

**Ask**

What is the final difference, and why do we add 200 and 40?

**Pause**

Let students state the calculation and explain that both changes widen the same gap.

**Say**

Removing 200 from the first shelf widens the gap by 200. Adding 40 to the second widens it by another 40. The final difference is 200 plus 40, or 240 books.

**Screen check**

The stage shows “240-book difference” and `200 + 40 = 240`, while both physical changes remain visible.

## Stop 5: Treat the first shelf as one part

**Action**

Click Next. Point only to the books that remain on the first shelf.

**Ask**

How many parts should the changed first shelf represent?

**Pause**

Wait for “one part.”

**Say**

We do not need to guess the number of books yet. Treat all the books left on the first shelf as one part and use that as our comparison unit.

**Screen check**

The remaining books on the first shelf are labeled “1 part.” The complete answer is still hidden.

## Stop 6: Build three equal parts on the second shelf

**Action**

Click Next. Point from bottom to top across the three equal rows on the second shelf.

**Ask**

If the second shelf has three times as many, how many equal parts should it have?

**Pause**

Wait for students to describe three parts equal in size to the first shelf’s part.

**Say**

The first shelf is one part. Three times that amount is three equal parts, so each row on the second shelf represents one matching part.

**Screen check**

The first shelf shows one part, the second shows three one-part labels, and the middle reads `1 : 3`. The shells do not move.

## Stop 7: Find the two extra parts

**Action**

Click Next. Point to the one part both sides share, then to the two extra rows on the second shelf.

**Ask**

How many more parts does the second shelf have?

**Pause**

Wait for `3 − 1 = 2`.

**Say**

The second shelf has three parts and the first has one. After matching the shared part, two extra parts remain. Those two parts make the difference.

**Screen check**

The two extra parts are highlighted, and the stage shows “2 extra parts” with `3 − 1 = 2`.

## Stop 8: Match two parts to 240 books

**Action**

Click Next. Connect the “2 parts = 240 books” brace to the highlighted extra rows.

**Ask**

Which visible section matches the 240-book difference?

**Pause**

Wait for students to point to the two extra parts on the second shelf.

**Say**

The two changes gave us a 240-book difference, and the multiple gave us a two-part difference. These are two ways of describing the same gap.

**Screen check**

The stage shows `2 parts = 240 books` and keeps the two extra parts highlighted.

## Stop 9: Find 120 books in each part

**Action**

Click Next. Point to `240 ÷ 2 = 120`, then to the “120 books” label in each part.

**Ask**

If 240 books are shared equally between two parts, how many books are in one part?

**Pause**

Wait for the calculation and an explanation of what 240 and 2 represent.

**Say**

The 240-book difference contains two equal parts. Dividing 240 by 2 gives 120 books in each part. The first changed shelf has one part, or 120 books, and the second has three parts, or 360 books.

**Screen check**

Every part is labeled 120 books, and the equation is `240 ÷ 2 = 120`. The original count of 320 is still hidden.

## Stop 10: Restore the removed 200 books

**Action**

Click Next. Watch the books fill the hatched space on the first shelf, then point to the reverse calculation.

**Ask**

The first shelf has 120 books after the change. How many did it have at first?

**Pause**

Wait for students to add back the 200 removed books.

**Say**

The 120 books remain after 200 were removed. To return to the starting amount, restore those 200 books: 120 plus 200 is 320.

**Screen check**

The first shelf returns to its starting height and the stage shows `120 + 200 = 320`. The second shelf still shows its changed amount.

## Stop 11: Restore the equal start

**Action**

Click Next. Point to the two equal book amounts and the equality sign in the middle.

**Ask**

If the first shelf started with 320 books, how many did the second shelf start with?

**Pause**

Wait for students to use the given equal start.

**Say**

The problem says the shelves started equal. Since the first started with 320 books, the second also started with 320 books.

**Screen check**

Both shelves return to equal starting amounts, with “Equal at the start” and `320 = 320`. The final answer sentence is not yet visible.

## Stop 12: Check the first change

**Action**

Click Next. Point to the space created on the first shelf, then to the first check.

**Ask**

Does removing 200 from 320 give 120?

**Pause**

Let students calculate and confirm.

**Say**

320 minus 200 is 120, exactly the one-part amount we found. The first change checks out.

**Screen check**

The stage shows `320 − 200 = 120` with the first green check. The other checks are not all complete.

## Stop 13: Check the second change

**Action**

Click Next. Point to the gold books added to the second shelf, then to the second check.

**Ask**

Does adding 40 to 320 give 360?

**Pause**

Let students calculate and confirm.

**Say**

320 plus 40 is 360, so the second change also checks out.

**Screen check**

The stage shows `320 + 40 = 360` with the first two green checks. The final answer remains hidden.

## Stop 14: Check the three-times relationship

**Action**

Click Next. Point to `1 : 3`, then to the third check.

**Ask**

How many times 120 is 360?

**Pause**

Wait for `360 ÷ 120 = 3`.

**Say**

360 divided by 120 is 3, so the second changed shelf really does have three times as many books. Every condition matches.

**Screen check**

All three checks are visible: `320 − 200 = 120`, `320 + 40 = 360`, and `360 ÷ 120 = 3`. The answer sentence is still hidden.

## Stop 15: State the complete answer

**Action**

Click Next. Point to the full sentence above the green underline and remain on this screen to close the explanation.

**Ask**

All conditions match. What is the complete answer?

**Pause**

Invite students to answer in a complete sentence first.

**Say**

Answer: Each shelf originally had 320 books.

**Screen check**

The final answer appears only at this stop. The main button changes to “Replay,” and clicking the open stage does not restart the lesson.

## Parameter branches

Use this section only after clicking “Edit problem,” and read only the branch that matches the current screen.

- **Valid whole-number branch:** The page adds “removed + added” to find the gap, subtracts 1 from the multiple to find the extra parts, then recomputes one part and the starting amount. Applying the values returns to Stop 1 without changing the 15-stop structure.
- **No whole-number branch:** If the gap cannot be split evenly among “multiple minus 1” parts, the dialog explains that there is no whole-book answer and prevents misleading lesson steps.
- **Large-multiple branch:** For a large multiple, the scene keeps the realistic bookcase shell and summarizes the number of parts instead of creating unreadably thin rows. Use the values shown on screen.
- **Replay behavior:** Stop 15 returns to Stop 1 only through the explicit Replay button, preventing an accidental stage click from restarting the lesson.
