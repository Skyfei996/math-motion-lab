# “A Rectangle Becomes a Square” Speaker Script

- Grade: Grade 4
- Lesson path: `lessons/长方形变成正方形/index.html`
- Suggested duration: 25–35 minutes
- Default values: rectangle area 225 square metres; divide the length by 5 and multiply the width by 5
- Teacher cues: Ask the question and pause before explaining each stop. Select “Next” to continue. “Change problem” stays in the upper-right toolbar and restarts the lesson at page 1. If values are changed, read the dynamic numbers shown on screen instead of the defaults below.

## Stop 1: Read the problem

**Action**

Open the lesson and stay on page 1, “Read.”

**Ask**

What happens to the length, and what happens to the width?

**Pause**

Wait for “divide the length by 5” and “multiply the width by 5.”

**Say**

The rectangle has an area of 225 square metres. We do not know its original length or width. We only know how both sides change before the shape becomes a square. Let us draw those changes before calculating.

**Screen check**

The page shows 225 square metres, length ÷ 5, width × 5, and an unknown square. It does not reveal the side length.

## Stop 2: Cut into strips

**Action**

Select “Next” and watch the teal dashed cut lines appear one after another.

**Ask**

If the length is split into 5 equal parts, how long is each part?

**Pause**

Accept “the original length divided by 5.” A numerical length is not needed yet.

**Say**

We can draw “length divided by 5” by cutting the rectangle into 5 equal pieces along its length. Each piece is one fifth of the original length. We have not moved, added, or removed any piece yet.

**Screen check**

The rectangle is divided into 5 pieces numbered 1 through 5.

## Stop 3: Focus on one part

**Action**

Select “Next.” Piece 1 turns gold while every piece stays in its original position.

**Ask**

The original length was split into 5 equal parts. What fraction of the original length is the gold part?

**Pause**

Accept “one fifth,” then ask for the calculation words “original length divided by 5.”

**Say**

Do not move anything yet. The gold strip is still the same piece; it has not suddenly become shorter or wider. When the original length is split into 5 equal parts, one part has a length of “original length ÷ 5.” This shows the first change in the problem.

**Screen check**

It is still the same rectangle. Only piece 1 is gold, and the working says “original length ÷ 5 = gold strip length.”

## Stop 4: Move the strips

**Action**

Select “Next” and watch the other 4 strips move under the gold strip one by one.

**Ask**

When 5 original widths are joined, how many times the original width is the new vertical side?

**Pause**

Wait for “5 times.”

**Say**

The width of each strip stays the same. The gold strip counts as one original width. Each arriving strip adds one more width. The final vertical side contains 5 original widths, so it represents “original width × 5.” The pieces move; they are not stretched or created.

**Screen check**

The same 5 pieces move on the same stage and fill one square outline. The width counter grows from 1 width to 5 widths.

## Stop 5: Keep the same area

**Action**

Select “Next” and compare the two arrangements.

**Ask**

Did we add or remove any piece when we cut and rearranged the rectangle?

**Pause**

Wait while students count 5 pieces in each arrangement.

**Say**

The shape changed, but the same 5 pieces are still there. None was added and none was removed. We only changed their positions, so the total area is still 225 square metres.

**Screen check**

Both arrangements show 5 pieces. The working states that both areas equal 225 square metres.

## Stop 6: Work from 1 to 10

**Action**

Select “Next” and let 1×1 through 10×10 appear in order.

**Ask**

If a square has side 1, 2, 3, and so on through 10, what is each area?

**Pause**

Let students calculate each product. If one is unfamiliar, picture equal rows with the same number in each row.

**Say**

The area of a square is side × side. Start small: 1×1=1, 2×2=4, 3×3=9, and continue to 10×10=100. The products keep growing, but 100 is still below 225, so the side must be greater than 10.

**Screen check**

The page shows the 10 products from 1 through 10 in increasing order.

## Stop 7: Continue from 11 to 20

**Action**

Select “Next” and inspect 11×11 through 20×20.

**Ask**

Starting at 11, when do we reach exactly 225?

**Pause**

Read 121, 144, 169, 196, and 225 in order. Stop at 15 and ask whether the search needs to continue.

**Say**

11×11=121, 12×12=144, 13×13=169, and 14×14=196 are below 225. Then 15×15=225 exactly, so the side is 15 metres. Since 16×16=256 is already above 225, every later product will also be too large.

**Screen check**

The page shows the 10 products from 11 through 20. The match is marked in green, while later products are muted as “too far.”

## Stop 8: Narrow the range first

**Action**

Select “Next” and look at 10, 15, and 20 on the range line.

**Ask**

If 10×10=100 is below 225 and 20×20=400 is above 225, must we still test from 1?

**Pause**

Wait for “No; search only from 10 to 20.”

**Say**

Use familiar tens as two gates. Ten squared is 100 on the left, and twenty squared is 400 on the right. Since 225 is between those areas, its side is between 10 and 20. Many numbers are removed before we calculate any candidates.

**Screen check**

The range line marks 10, 15, and 20, and the working shows 10×10=100 and 20×20=400.

## Stop 9: Check the ones digit

**Action**

Select “Next” and inspect the ones-digit results for 0 through 9 multiplied by themselves.

**Ask**

The area ends in 5. Which ones digit gives 5 when multiplied by itself?

**Pause**

Let students inspect the cells and answer “5.” Then ask which number from 10 to 20 ends in 5.

**Say**

Look only at the ones digit: 5×5=25. A square ending in 5 suggests a side ending in 5. In our narrowed range, only 15 ends in 5. This is a filter, not proof; we still calculate 15×15.

**Screen check**

The digit 5 and candidate 15 are highlighted; the other cells remain neutral.

## Stop 10: Calculate 15×15 clearly

**Action**

Select “Next.” The same square is divided into a 15×10 part and a 15×5 part.

**Ask**

If 15×15 is unfamiliar, can one 15 be split into 10 and 5?

**Pause**

Calculate 15×10, then 15×5, then add the two results.

**Say**

Think of 15×15 as 15 rows of 15. Split each row into 10 and 5. The first part is 15×10=150, and the second is 15×5=75. Together, 150+75=225. We have now calculated the match instead of merely guessing it.

**Screen check**

The same square is divided into a gold part and a teal part. The calculation card shows 150+75=225.

## Stop 11: Answer the original problem

**Action**

Select “Next,” read the full answer, and then continue with “Next.” Do not look for a branch button.

**Ask**

What are the square's area and side length?

**Pause**

Ask for a complete sentence and listen for the two different units.

**Say**

The square's area is 225 square metres, and its side length is 15 metres. Square metres measure area, while metres measure length. The original question is answered; the next pages recover and check the original rectangle.

**Screen check**

The page shows 225 square metres and 15 metres. It has no separate reverse, explore, or change-problem button.

## Stop 12: Recover the original length

**Action**

Select “Next” and watch the strips travel back from the square to the rectangle.

**Ask**

Fifteen metres came from dividing the original length by 5. How do we reverse that?

**Pause**

Wait for “15 times 5.”

**Say**

The original length divided by 5 became 15 metres. Reverse the change by multiplying 15 metres by 5. Fifteen times five is 75, so the original length was 75 metres.

**Screen check**

The strips move continuously back into a rectangle, and the working shows 15 × 5 = 75 metres.

## Stop 13: Recover the original width

**Action**

Select “Next” and point to the highlighted short side of the rectangle.

**Ask**

Fifteen metres came from multiplying the original width by 5. How do we reverse that?

**Pause**

Wait for “15 divided by 5.”

**Say**

The original width multiplied by 5 became 15 metres. Split 15 metres into 5 equal parts. Fifteen divided by five is 3, so the original width was 3 metres.

**Screen check**

The working shows 15 ÷ 5 = 3 metres, and the short side is labelled 3 metres.

## Stop 14: Check backwards

**Action**

Select “Next” and work through the three checks: area, new length, and new width.

**Ask**

Do 75 metres and 3 metres give both the correct area and the required square?

**Pause**

Let students calculate 75 × 3, then 75 ÷ 5 and 3 × 5.

**Say**

First check the area: 75 times 3 is 225, matching the given area. Then check the changes: 75 divided by 5 is 15, and 3 times 5 is 15. The new sides are equal, so the rectangle really does become a square.

**Screen check**

The page shows 75 × 3 = 225, 75 ÷ 5 = 15, 3 × 5 = 15, and two check marks.

## Stop 15: Use the method after changing the problem

**Action**

Select “Next” and point through “Find range → Check ones → Multiply back.” Selecting the final button again starts over.

**Ask**

Why is it faster to estimate the number of digits, check the ones digit, and then verify?

**Pause**

Ask students to repeat the three steps and explain what each step removes.

**Say**

First use familiar tens or hundreds multiplied by themselves to narrow the side to a small range. Next, use the area's ones digit to remove impossible candidates. Finally, multiply the remaining candidates back to verify. The order stays the same after changing the values. Not every area has a whole-number side; the change-problem dialog explains when a value is unsuitable.

**Screen check**

The 2-digit band is highlighted, and the method track shows the three steps in order.

## Change-problem branch

**Action**

At any time, select “Change problem,” enter an area and a change number from 2 to 10, and select “Apply and restart.”

**Say**

- If the page says the area is not a whole number multiplied by itself, try 144, 225, or 400.
- If the original width would be a decimal, change the factor or use an area whose side divides evenly by that factor.
- Check case: area 144 and factor 3 gives a 12-metre square, an original length of 36 metres, and an original width of 4 metres.
- Check case: area 400 and factor 4 gives a 20-metre square, an original length of 80 metres, and an original width of 5 metres.
