# Chickens and Rabbits — English Speaker Script

- Grade: Grade 4
- Lesson path: `lessons/鸡兔同笼/index.html`
- Suggested duration: 12–15 minutes
- Default values: 8 heads and 22 legs
- Teacher note: Use only “Previous” and “Next” to present the lesson. Ask the question and pause before selecting “Next.” Text in square brackets is a teacher cue and is not meant to be read aloud.

## Stop 1: Start with heads and legs

**Action**

Open the lesson and stay on page 1. To use different values, select “Edit problem,” enter the totals, and select “Apply and restart.”

**Ask**

There are 8 heads and 22 legs. What are we trying to find?

**Pause**

Wait for students to say that we need the number of chickens and rabbits.

**Say**

We know there are 8 animals because every animal has one head. We do not yet know which animals are chickens and which are rabbits, so the screen shows only eight one-head symbols. It does not guess the answer.

For a very large total, the page groups animals automatically. Read the caption, such as “Each symbol represents 50 animals.” If there is a remainder, the final symbol shows its exact amount.

**Screen check**

The default problem shows 8 heads and 22 legs. No chicken or rabbit answer is visible.

## Stop 2: Give every head 2 legs

**Action**

Select “Next.”

**Ask**

If every animal were a chicken, how many legs would there be?

**Pause**

Wait for students to calculate `8 × 2`.

**Say**

Assume every animal is a chicken. Put 2 legs under every head. Eight heads receive 16 legs.

Those 16 legs are not imaginary. Whether an animal is really a chicken or a rabbit, it has at least those first 2 legs. We have given every animal its basic pair.

**Screen check**

The blackboard shows `8 × 2 = 16`, and every animal structure has one head and two legs.

## Stop 3: Find the extra legs

**Action**

Select “Next.”

**Ask**

There are 22 actual legs, but we placed only 16. How many legs are still missing?

**Pause**

Wait for students to calculate `22 − 16`.

**Say**

There are 22 legs in the problem and 16 legs in our all-chicken picture. The difference is 6. The screen marks those six as extra legs.

The extra legs do not have owners yet. The important next question is why these legs tell us how many rabbits there are.

**Screen check**

The screen shows 22 actual legs, 16 assumed legs, and 6 extra legs. The blackboard shows `22 − 16 = 6`.

## Stop 4: A rabbit adds 2, not 4

**Action**

Select “Next.”

**Ask**

After both animals have already received 2 legs, how many more does a chicken need? How many more does a rabbit need?

**Pause**

Let students compare the two structures and notice the two colors on the rabbit's legs.

**Say**

A chicken needs 2 legs. It already has 2, so it needs no more.

A rabbit needs 4 legs, but it also received 2 legs during the all-chicken step. The rabbit does not still need 4 legs. It needs only 2 more.

This is why we do not divide by 4. A rabbit's 4 legs contain two parts: 2 legs already counted and 2 extra legs. We are working with the extra legs, not all the rabbit's legs.

**Screen check**

The chicken structure has one head and two legs. The rabbit structure has one head and four legs, with the two added legs highlighted. The blackboard shows `Chicken: 2−2=0` and `Rabbit: 4−2=2`.

## Stop 5: Group the extra legs in pairs

**Action**

Select “Next.”

**Ask**

How many pairs can we make from 6 extra legs?

**Pause**

Wait for students to say 3 pairs.

**Say**

Put the 6 extra legs into groups of 2. That makes 3 groups, so `6 ÷ 2 = 3 groups`.

At this point, dividing by 2 counts groups of extra legs. We are not dividing by all 4 legs of a rabbit.

For a large problem, one symbol may represent many groups. For example, a symbol marked 50 contains fifty separate two-leg groups. The relationship stays the same.

**Screen check**

The default problem shows three two-leg groups. The blackboard shows `6 ÷ 2 = 3 pairs`.

**Conditional branch: no extra legs**

If actual legs equal heads times 2, the page shows 0 extra legs and 0 groups. Explain that every animal was completed with its first 2 legs, so no animal needs an extra pair.

## Stop 6: One extra pair means one rabbit

**Action**

Select “Next.”

**Ask**

Why does extra legs divided by 2 give the number of rabbits?

**Pause**

Ask students to describe the link between one pair of legs and one rabbit.

**Say**

After receiving its first 2 legs, each rabbit needs exactly 2 more. Take one extra pair and add it to one assumed chicken. The one-head, two-leg structure becomes a one-head, four-leg structure: a rabbit.

One pair belongs to exactly one rabbit. Three pairs therefore identify three rabbits. This is a one-to-one match.

The complete rule is: rabbits equal extra legs divided by the number of legs each rabbit has beyond a chicken. In the default problem, `6 ÷ 2 = 3 rabbits`.

Emphasize this sentence: We divide by 2 not because a rabbit has 2 legs, but because each rabbit has 2 more legs than the chicken we assumed.

**Screen check**

The page shows “one extra pair → one rabbit.” The blackboard shows `Rabbits = extra legs ÷ extra legs per rabbit` and `6 ÷ 2 = 3`.

**Large-number branch**

For 10,000 heads and 30,000 legs, all chickens would have 20,000 legs. The extra 10,000 legs give `10,000 ÷ 2 = 5,000 rabbits`. If each visual group represents 50 animals, one rabbit group needs `50 × 2 = 100` extra legs. One hundred such groups still represent 5,000 rabbits.

## Stop 7: The rest are chickens

**Action**

Select “Next.”

**Ask**

There are 8 animals and 3 are rabbits. How many chickens remain?

**Pause**

Wait for students to calculate `8 − 3`.

**Say**

The total has always been 8 animals. We found 3 rabbits, so the remaining 5 animals are chickens.

The page uses yellow one-head, two-leg structures for chickens and blue one-head, four-leg structures for rabbits. We now have both intermediate counts, but we will state the complete answer only after checking it on the next page.

**Screen check**

The blackboard shows `8 − 3 = 5`. The stage shows 5 chickens and 3 rabbits but no complete answer sentence.

**Conditional branch: all rabbits or all chickens**

If the chicken count is 0, every animal needed an extra pair. If the rabbit count is 0, no animal needed an extra pair. Both are valid problems.

## Stop 8: Both totals must match

**Action**

Select “Next.” After this page, the button changes to “Replay”; select it to return to page 1.

**Ask**

Do the calculated chickens and rabbits match both totals in the problem?

**Pause**

Check heads first, then legs.

**Say**

We found 5 chickens and 3 rabbits. Check the heads: `5 + 3 = 8`, so the head total matches.

Now check the legs. Five chickens have `5 × 2 = 10` legs. Three rabbits have `3 × 4 = 12` legs. Together, `10 + 12 = 22`, so the leg total also matches.

Both checks pass, so we can give the complete answer: 5 chickens and 3 rabbits.

**Screen check**

The default problem shows two green checks and the final sentence “Answer: 5 chickens and 3 rabbits.” This is the first page that displays the complete answer.
