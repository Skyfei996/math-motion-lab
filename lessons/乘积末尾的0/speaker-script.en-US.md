# Trailing Zeros in a Product — Speaker Script

- Grade: Grades 4–5
- Lesson path: `lessons/乘积末尾的0/index.html`
- Suggested duration: 15–20 minutes
- Default values: multiply 45; require at least 2 trailing zeros
- Teacher cue: Instructions under **Action** are for the presenter. Pause before moving on. The star icon represents a positive whole number; it is not an emoji to be read aloud.

## Stop 1: Multiply by 5

**Action**

Open the lesson and stay on page 1, “Multiply by 5.” Point across the ten cards from 1 × 5 through 10 × 5.

**Ask**

Which products end in zero? What digit appears at the end of the other products?

**Pause**

Wait for students to name at least two products ending in zero and one counterexample.

**Say**

We should not select only the examples that work. One times five is five, so it does not end in zero. Two times five is ten, so it does. As we continue, the final digit alternates between five and zero. When the other number has an even ones digit—0, 2, 4, 6, or 8—the product ends in zero. With an odd ones digit, it ends in five.

**Screen check**

The page shows 1 through 10 multiplied by 5. Green cards emphasize a final zero; red counterexample cards emphasize a final five. The value of the star is not shown.

## Stop 2: Move the pattern to larger numbers

**Action**

Select “Next.” Point to the three groups headed “Ones digit 2,” “Ones digit 0,” and “Counterexamples: ones digit 1.”

**Ask**

Why do 12, 22, and 32 all end in zero after multiplying by 5? What do 10, 20, 30, and 40 have in common?

**Pause**

Wait for students to identify the ones digits 2 and 0.

**Say**

Twelve, twenty-two, and thirty-two all have 2 in the ones place. Ten, twenty, thirty, and forty all have 0 in the ones place, and zero is even. They follow the same rule. The counterexamples 11, 21, and 31 all have a ones digit of 1, so their products end in five. The size of the number is not what matters here; the ones digit is.

**Screen check**

The page shows the requested examples with 12, 22, 32, 10, 20, 30, and 40, plus counterexamples using 11, 21, and 31.

## Stop 3: Why the ones digit is enough

**Action**

Select “Next.” Point first to the highlighted 2 in 32, then to the “Tens part” and “Ones part” panels.

**Ask**

Why can 30 × 5 not change the product's ones digit to something other than zero?

**Pause**

Wait for an answer such as, “Thirty times a whole number is still a multiple of ten.”

**Say**

We can see 32 as 30 and 2. Thirty times five is 150, which is still a multiple of ten. Two times five is ten, and that decides the final digit. When we are deciding only the last digit of a product, the two ones digits are enough. This does not mean the other place values are unimportant; it means they cannot change the ones place.

**Screen check**

The page shows 32 × 5 = 30 × 5 + 2 × 5 = 150 + 10 = 160 and compares 45 × 11 with 45 × 12.

## Stop 4: Lock the first zero

**Action**

Select “Next.” For the default problem, point to 45 × 1 and 45 × 2, then point to the first slot in the “Zero lock track.”

**Ask**

What is the smallest number that 45 can be multiplied by to make its first trailing zero?

**Pause**

Let students predict before comparing the two trial cards.

**Say**

The ones digit of 45 is 5. Forty-five times one is still 45. Forty-five times two is 90, the first result ending in zero, so two is the smallest choice this round. Lock the zero in 90 and continue with the 9 in front. Locking the zero means keeping it safe, not deleting it.

After the values are changed, read only the branch shown on screen: an active number already ending in zero locks it immediately; an active ones digit of 5 tries through 2; 2, 4, 6, or 8 tries through 5; and 1, 3, 7, or 9 tries through 10.

**Screen check**

For the default problem, the page shows 45 × 1 = 45 and 45 × 2 = 90. The track changes from 0/2 to 1/2. The answer 20 is still hidden.

## Stop 5: Lock the second zero

**Action**

Select “Next.” For the default problem, scan the cards from 9 × 1 through 9 × 10, then point to the second locked zero.

**Ask**

The first zero is safe. Looking only at the 9 in front, what is the smallest new multiplier that makes the second zero?

**Pause**

Pause after 9 × 9 so students can notice that none of the first nine trials ends in zero.

**Say**

Nine times each number from one through nine ends in a digit other than zero. Nine times ten is 90, the first success. The smallest choice in this round is ten. Lock that zero, and the target of two zeros is complete.

When a changed problem needs more zeros, the page creates another round for each one. If the target is greater than twelve, later repeated rounds are grouped on one page; their calculation is still included.

**Screen check**

For the default problem, only 9 × 10 is marked “first success,” and the zero track reads 2/2.

## Stop 6: Combine the smallest choices

**Action**

Select “Next.” Point to “Zero 1: ×2” and “Zero 2: ×10.” Do not advance yet.

**Ask**

If we first multiply by 2 and then by 10, what is the combined multiplier? Why have we not skipped a smaller choice?

**Pause**

Let students calculate 2 × 10 mentally while the page continues to hide the answer.

**Say**

The first round began at one and stopped when two first worked. The second round also began at one and stopped when ten first worked. Every smaller trial was checked at each stage, so combining those first successful choices does not skip a smaller answer. Keep the result in mind and use the next page to multiply it back by 45.

**Screen check**

The page shows the two round choices and “2 × 10 = ?” The star still contains a question mark; neither 20 nor 900 is visible.

## Stop 7: Check and change the problem

**Action**

Select “Next” to reveal the result. After completing the default problem, select “Change the problem,” enter new values, and select “Apply and restart.”

**Ask**

Does 45 × 20 meet the target of at least two zeros? Why is 20 the smallest value?

**Pause**

Wait for students to mention both facts: 900 has two trailing zeros, and each round tested every smaller choice first.

**Say**

Multiplying first by two and then by ten is the same as multiplying once by twenty. Forty-five times twenty is 900, which has two trailing zeros. Every smaller option in both rounds was already ruled out, so the smallest positive whole number represented by the star is twenty. With new values, look at the active ones digit again after every zero is locked; do not keep looking only at the original ones digit.

**Screen check**

For the first time, the page shows the star equal to 20 and 45 × 20 = 900, with the final two zeros highlighted. The “Change the problem” button opens the settings dialog.
